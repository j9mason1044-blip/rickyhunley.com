Leadership

What the huddle teaches that the meeting room can’t

Eleven people. One voice. Four seconds to decide. Why the huddle is still the best leadership lab I know.

No slide deck. No follow-up email. No “let’s circle back.” Just a call, a break, and eleven people trusting each other enough to move as one on a ball they can’t see yet.

I’ve sat in a lot of meeting rooms since I hung up my pads — boardrooms, coaches’ offices, donor lunches, committee tables. Some of the smartest people I know run those rooms. But I’ve never seen a meeting room produce what a huddle produces, and after almost fifty years around this game, I think I finally know why.

A huddle has no room for pretending.

In a meeting, you can nod along. You can stay quiet and let it look like agreement. A huddle won’t let you hide. If you don’t know the play, your body gives you away before the ball is even snapped. The huddle exposes preparation instantly — and it exposes trust just as fast. You either believe the voice calling the play, or you don’t. There’s no time to build that belief in the huddle itself. You bring it in with you, built from every rep before that moment.

A huddle has one voice, not the loudest voice.

Somebody calls the play. Everybody else’s job is to execute it with everything they’ve got, even if they’d have called something different. That’s not blind obedience — it’s discipline. It’s understanding that the team needs unity of action more than it needs eleven opinions. I learned leadership in the huddle before I ever learned it from a title. The quarterback wasn’t always the best athlete on the field. He was the one everybody believed would get it right, and who took responsibility when it went wrong.

A huddle ends in commitment, not consensus.

Four seconds, then everybody breaks toward the same purpose. No further debate at the line. That’s the piece I think we’ve lost in a lot of our institutions — the willingness to commit fully to a decision once it’s made, instead of relitigating it play after play, meeting after meeting.

I still run my days like a huddle when I can. Say it plainly. Trust the people around you. Commit without flinching. Then go execute — together.

That’s not nostalgia talking. That’s the best leadership lab I ever stood in, and I’m still teaching from what it showed me.
