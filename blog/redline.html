Redline: how Coach Brennan’s standard echoes seven hard lessons

Effort, execution, accountability. It’s the standard I was chasing my whole career — I just didn’t have the word for it.

When Brent Brennan first brought “Redline” to Tucson, he borrowed the term from racing. On a car, redline is the point on the tachometer where the engine is running at its absolute limit — the edge of maximum output before something breaks. Brennan’s version isn’t about breaking anything. It’s about living there anyway: full effort, full execution, full accountability, every single rep. Coach has said it plainly — Redline comes down to effort, execution, and accountability. No more, no less.

I didn’t have a one-word name for it when I played. But reading back through the seven lessons I wrote for my younger self, I realize Redline was the standard I was reaching for the entire time — I just didn’t have the language for it yet. Here’s how I see the two connect.

Lesson 1 — Knowing is not enough, we must apply.

This is Redline in its purest form. Knowing the playbook, knowing the standard, knowing what’s required — none of it means anything until you apply it at full speed, on every rep, whether anyone’s grading you or not. Redline doesn’t have an off switch for the plays nobody’s watching.

Lesson 2 — Embrace coaching.

Execution and accountability are team words, not individual ones. You can’t hold yourself to Redline in isolation — it requires being coachable enough to hear where you fell short and humble enough to fix it before the next rep. A player who can’t take coaching can’t sustain Redline; he just has good days.

Lesson 3 — You are only a hero for a moment.

Redline isn’t a highlight-reel standard. It’s not one big play — it’s the discipline underneath a hundred quiet ones. The players who chase the “hero moment” instead of the standard will flame out fast. The ones who live at Redline build something that outlasts any single game.

Lesson 4 — Those who stay the course will be champions.

I started sixth on the depth chart and stayed the course until I was starting every game of my career. That’s Redline over time — not one explosive week, but sustained effort until the results catch up to the standard. Coach Brennan’s teams that have found their “Mr. Redlines” are the ones who kept applying the standard through fall camp, through the bye week, through the grind nobody sees on Saturdays.

Lesson 5 — Humble yourself, think before you speak.

Accountability is the third leg of Redline, and accountability starts with how you handle being wrong. It’s owning your mistakes out loud instead of shifting blame, and it’s understanding that your words in the locker room or in front of a microphone reflect on the whole standard, not just you.

Lesson 6 — Give back.

Redline isn’t sustainable as a solo pursuit. The reason a program can hold 105 players to one standard is that the older guys — the Mr. Redlines — pass it down to the young ones. That’s coaching in its truest form, and it’s exactly what my mother taught me about giving your time, not just your talent, to the people coming up behind you.

Lesson 7 — Think before you act, be all that you can be.

This is the lesson I regret not living out fully as a player — the discipline in the weight room, the recovery, the extra rep nobody’s counting. Redline demands exactly that: the “above and beyond” work that separates good from great, applied consistently enough that it becomes who you are, not just how you play on Saturday.

Redline is a new word for an old truth. Effort, execution, accountability — that’s the standard I was chasing my whole career, whether I had the vocabulary for it or not. What I love about what Coach Brennan has built at Arizona is that it’s not just a slogan on a locker room wall. It’s a shared language for exactly what I was trying to teach my younger self all those years later: the standard doesn’t move. You either rise to meet it, or you don’t.

Bear Down, and stay at Redline.
