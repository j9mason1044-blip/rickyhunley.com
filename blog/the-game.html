The game

Life after the snap

The transition nobody drafts you for, and what athletes should be building long before it arrives.

Nobody drafts you for this transition. There’s no combine for it, no scouting report, no agent negotiating the terms. One day the game is the organizing principle of your entire life, and the next, it isn’t — and almost nobody prepares an athlete for that particular silence.

I played this game at the highest level. Two Super Bowls. A first-round life built around being one of the best at something very specific and very physical. And then, like it does for every single player eventually, it ended. Not on my terms. It rarely is on anyone’s terms.

Here’s what I want young athletes to hear, long before they’re anywhere near that moment: The identity you build now is the one you’ll need later.

If “athlete” is the only word that describes you, retirement doesn’t just end a career — it ends you, or at least it feels that way. The players I’ve watched struggle most after the game are the ones who never developed a second identity while the first one was still paying the bills. Start building the second one now. Coach a youth team. Learn a trade. Get curious about business, ministry, teaching, whatever pulls at you. Do it while football is still giving you the platform and the resources to explore.

Your network shouldn’t be limited to the field.

Locker rooms are tight-knit for good reason — but they can also become a bubble. The relationships that carry you through transition are the ones you built outside of football while you still had the game as your calling card. Community leaders, mentors, people ten and twenty years older than you who’ve already made a hard transition of their own. Build those bridges early. Don’t wait until you need them.

Purpose has to be bigger than performance.

Performance is intoxicating. The standing ovation, the highlight, the win — none of that translates cleanly to civilian life. What does translate is purpose: who you’re trying to help, what you’re trying to build, what legacy you actually want. Start asking that question at nineteen, not thirty-nine.

I didn’t have this roadmap when my own transition came. I’ve spent the years since building it — for myself, and now for the young men I get to mentor. The last snap is coming for every single athlete reading this. The only question is whether you’re building something now that will still be standing when it does.
